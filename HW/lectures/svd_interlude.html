SVD Interlude

1 A Geometric Buildup to the SVD

1.1 From Input/Output Spaces to Orthogonal Directions That Stay Orthogonal

1.1.1 Big Idea

A matrix does not always represent motion within a space.

Often it represents a process that takes in one kind of data and produces another.

Examples:

Input Output
3D position 2D screen coordinates
RGB color (3 numbers) grayscale brightness (1 number)
3 forces on an object net torque (3 → 1)
5 features a prediction score

So a matrix can represent a transformation

\[ A: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m \]

The input space and output space may be different worlds.

. . .

1.2 A Concrete Example (3 → 2)

Consider

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ A(x,y,z) = (x,y). \]

. . .

Geometrically:

  • motion in the \(x\) direction shows up in the output
  • motion in the \(y\) direction shows up in the output
  • motion in the \(z\) direction is completely ignored

. . .

So:

  • the entire \(xy\)-plane survives
  • everything in the \(z\) direction gets squashed to zero

Already we see:

Some directions in the input space matter, and some directions don’t.

. . .

1.3 2. The “Visible Directions”

Test the standard basis vectors:

\[ Ae_1 = (1,0), \qquad Ae_2 = (0,1), \qquad Ae_3 = (0,0). \]

So:

  • motion in the \(e_1\) direction is visible
  • motion in the \(e_2\) direction is visible
  • motion in the \(e_3\) direction is invisible

This gives

\[ \ker(A) = \mathrm{span}{e_3}. \]

So the transformation:

  1. ignores motion in one direction
  2. does something with the rest

. . .

1.4 3. Decomposing Any Vector

Every vector \(x \in \mathbb{R}^3\) can be written as

\[ x = (\text{part in the }xy\text{-plane}) + (\text{part in the }z\text{-direction}). \]

Apply \(A\):

\[ Ax = A(\text{xy part}) + A(\text{z part}). \]

But the second term is always zero, so

\[ Ax = A(\text{xy part}). \]

Before doing anything interesting, a transformation throws away the part of the input it can’t see.

. . .

1.5 4. General Fact (No Proof Yet)

For any linear map

\[ A: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m \]

there are:

  • some directions it kills
  • some directions it responds to

In fact,

\[ \mathbb{R}^n = (\text{directions it responds to}) \oplus (\text{directions it kills}). \]

. . .

1.6 Interlude: More Outputs Than Inputs (2 → 3)

Now consider the opposite situation:

\[ A: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3. \]

Here there are more outputs than inputs, so it is impossible for (A) to fill all of (^3) — there just aren’t enough input directions to control all three output directions.

So instead of some input directions being killed, we now get:

the outputs are constrained to lie in a lower-dimensional slice of the output space.

. . .

1.7 Concrete Example

Let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \ 0 & 1 \ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Then

\[ A(x,y) = (x,y,0). \]

Geometrically:

  • every input point in (^2) gets mapped into (^3)
  • but it always lands in the (xy)-plane

So all outputs lie in a 2D subspace of (^3).

Notice that in this example, just like the earlier map \(\mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2\), we have

\[ \mathrm{rank}(A) = 2. \]

In both cases, the transformation can only act in two independent directions.

. . .

1.8 Even Lower Rank (Less Than Both (m) and (n))

It can also happen that the rank is smaller than both the input and output dimensions.

For instance, let

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3. \]

Then

\[ A(x,y) = (x+y,0,0). \]

Now:

  • all outputs lie along a line in (^3)
  • the map only uses one independent input direction

So

\[ \mathrm{rank}(A) = 1. \]

Geometrically:

the transformation responds to motion in only one direction and all outputs are constrained to a one-dimensional slice of the output space.

This will show up later as having only one nonzero singular value.

. . .

1.9 Takeaway

The number of independent directions the map can actually do anything with is its rank — regardless of input or output dimension.

1.10 5. Focus on the Surviving Subspace

Call the surviving directions

\[ (\ker A)^\perp. \]

These are exactly the directions where motion in the input produces motion in the output.

Let

\[ v_1,\dots,v_r \]

be an orthonormal basis for this subspace (so \(r = \mathrm{rank}(A)\)).

. . .

1.11 6. The SVD Motivation

Ask:

Can we choose input directions \(v_1,\dots,v_r\) so that their images [Av_1,,Av_r] are orthogonal in the output space?

That would mean the transformation treats these directions independently.

Define

\[ w_j = Av_j. \]

We want:

\[ w_i^T w_j = 0 \quad (i \ne j). \]

But

\[ w_i^T w_j = (Av_i)^T(Av_j) = v_i^T A^T A v_j. \]

So this will happen if we choose the \(v_j\) to be eigenvectors of \(A^T A\).

Let

\[ A^T A v_j = \sigma_j^2 v_j. \]

Then

\[ |w_j|^2 = (Av_j)^T(Av_j) = v_j^T A^T A v_j = \sigma_j^2. \]

So the outputs are orthogonal, with known lengths.

. . .

1.12 7. Normalize the Outputs

Define

\[ u_j = \frac{1}{\sigma_j} w_j = \frac{1}{\sigma_j} Av_j. \]

Then

  • \(u_1,\dots,u_r\) are orthonormal
  • and

\[ Av_j = \sigma_j u_j. \]

. . .

1.13 8. Matrix Form

Let

\[ V_r = [v_1\ \cdots\ v_r], \qquad U_r = [u_1\ \cdots\ u_r], \qquad \Sigma_r = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_r). \]

Then the relations \(Av_j = \sigma_j u_j\) become

\[ A V_r = U_r \Sigma_r. \]

. . .

1.14 9. Geometric Interpretation

For any input \(x\):

  1. express \(x\) in the orthonormal directions \(v_j\)
  2. scale each coordinate by \(\sigma_j\)
  3. rebuild the result in the orthonormal directions \(u_j\)

So, in the right orthonormal coordinates in the domain and codomain,

a linear transformation is just independent scalings.

. . .

1.15 10. Extending to Full Bases

Extend

  • \({v_1,\dots,v_r}\) to an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\)
  • \({u_1,\dots,u_r}\) to an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^m\)

Let

\[ V = [v_1\ \cdots\ v_n], \qquad U = [u_1\ \cdots\ u_m]. \]

Then with respect to these orthonormal coordinates,

\[ U^T A V = \Sigma \]

is diagonal, and hence

\[ A = U \Sigma V^T. \]

This is the Singular Value Decomposition.
